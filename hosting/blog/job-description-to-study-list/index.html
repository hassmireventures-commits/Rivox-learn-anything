Turn a Job Description into a Study List (Interview Practice)

Published: September 19, 2026

A job description is a messy hint, not a syllabus. If you try to "learn everything on the page," you will buy three courses and still freeze when someone asks "tell me about a time." The useful move is to translate the ad into a short list of skills you can practice out loud and on paper.

Read the ad in three passes

Pass 1: outcomes. Ignore tools for a minute. Highlight verbs tied to results: ship, debug, hire, forecast, negotiate, teach. Those are what the team will actually ask about.

Pass 2: must-haves vs wallpaper. Must-haves are repeated, listed under requirements, or named in the title. Wallpaper is a 20-item stack at the bottom. You do not need a certificate in every logo.

Pass 3: evidence you already have. Next to each must-have, write one proof: a project, a metric, a story, or "gap." Gaps become the study list. Proofs become interview answers.

Build a list of five, not fifty

Cap the study list at five skills for the next two weeks. Example for a backend role whose ad mentions APIs, Postgres, on-call, and "stakeholder communication":

  1. Explain how you would design a simple REST endpoint and what you would log.
  2. Walk through indexing a slow query in language a non-DBA can follow.
  3. Describe an incident: detect, mitigate, write the follow-up.
  4. Tell a story of a missed estimate and how you reset expectations.
  5. Answer one language-specific drill the company actually uses (if you can find it from public interview reports).

Everything else waits. You can mention extra tools in a sentence. You cannot practice twenty tools in a week.

Practice like the interview, not like a MOOC

For each of the five, write a 60 to 90 second spoken answer. Use a simple shape: context, what you did, what changed, what you would do next. Record yourself once. If you ramble past two minutes, cut the setup, not the result.

Then add a hostile follow-up you invent yourself: "What would you do if that metric was wrong?" or "Why not the other database?" If you cannot answer, that skill is still a gap. Reading a blog post is not the close. Closing is answering the follow-up without notes.

HR rounds still need this list. "Tell me about yourself" should mention two of the five skills in the company's language, not a life story. Mirror their verbs. If they say "customer obsession," do not only say "I like users."

When the ad is vague

Some posts are a pile of adjectives. Look at the company's product and recent news. Guess three problems the hire will face in the first 90 days. Practice those. You can confirm in the recruiter screen: "I assumed this role spends time on X. Is that fair?" That question is itself interview practice.

Frequently asked questions

Should I study every tool listed?

No. Study the tools that show up in must-have bullets and that you cannot explain in two minutes. Nice-to-have lists are often copied from other ads.

How long should this take before applying?

A first map can take 30 minutes. Daily practice is then 15 to 30 minutes on the top five gaps until you can tell a story for each.

What if I have never done the job?

Borrow stories from adjacent work: a class project, a volunteer role, a bug you fixed. Label them honestly. Interviewers care that you can reason, not that you invent tenure.
