Active Recall vs Rereading: Make a 10-Question Self-Quiz from Notes

Published: September 19, 2026

Rereading feels productive because the page looks familiar. Familiarity is not the same as being able to produce the idea with the book closed. Active recall is the unglamorous version: you ask a question, you try to answer, then you check. That gap is where learning happens.

Why testing yourself beats highlighting

Highlighter ink marks what caught your eye, not what you can retrieve. Retrieval is effortful. That effort is the point. When you pull a definition, a formula, or a cause-and-effect chain out of memory, you strengthen the path you will need in an exam or a meeting. When you only reread, you practice recognizing the author's words. Recognition is easier, so it fools you.

You do not need a lab to see this in your own week. Read a page twice. Close it. Write three facts without looking. Most people stall by fact two. That stall is useful data. It tells you what to study next, instead of giving you another yellow page.

Turn one page of notes into 10 questions

Use a single source: one lecture, one chapter section, or one meeting note. Do not mix three PDFs. Ten questions from a mixed pile become trivia.

  1. Skim headings and the last sentence of each paragraph. Those often hide the claims.
  2. Write 10 prompts on a blank page or in a notes app. Aim for a mix: 4 "what is", 3 "why / how", 2 "compare", 1 "apply to a new example".
  3. Hide the source. Answer in short sentences. Guessing counts. Blank also counts. Mark blanks as misses.
  4. Open the source. Score yourself. For each miss, write a corrected answer in your own words, not a copied sentence.
  5. Park the 10 prompts. Tomorrow, try them again before you read anything new.

Example from a notes line "osmosis is water moving across a membrane toward higher solute": a weak prompt is "define osmosis." A stronger prompt is "A cell is in salty water. Which way does water move, and why?" The second one forces you to use the idea.

Keep the quiz honest

Do not look at the next heading while you write an answer. Do not grade "close enough" if the exam will not. If a question only works when you peek at a diagram, redraw the diagram from memory as the answer. If you cannot write 10 items, you do not understand the page yet. Reread once with a purpose: find claims, then close the book again.

Multiple-choice can still be active recall if you cover the options first and predict the answer. Then uncover and see if you were right. The prediction is the work. Picking C because it "looks right" is rereading in disguise.

What to do with the misses

Misses are the study list. Copy them to a running "can't retrieve yet" note. Do not rebuild a full 10-item quiz on a topic you already scored 9/10. Add two new questions from the next page instead. Once a week, shuffle old misses so you are not only practicing them in the original order.

Frequently asked questions

Is highlighting ever useful?

Yes, as a first pass to mark what you will later turn into questions. Highlighting is not the review. The review is answering those questions with the notes closed.

Should the 10 questions be multiple choice?

Short answer and closed-book recall are stronger. Use multiple choice only when the real exam uses it, and still write a one-line reason after each pick.

What if I cannot invent 10 questions?

Start with headings. Turn each heading into "what does this mean" and "when would I use this." Five honest questions beat ten copied ones.
